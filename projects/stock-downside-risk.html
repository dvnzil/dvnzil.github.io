Predicting the Unknown Drop

Are short-term stock drops unpredictable, or do measurable signals appear before downside risk rises? Modeling the magnitude of five-day downside risk for AAPL, MSFT, NVDA, GOOG, and AMZN — and finding that a linear model beat both ensembles tested against it.

The anxiety of the unknown drop

Most people treat the stock market as a guess about direction — will it go up or down. For an everyday investor, the sharper fear isn't a missed gain. It's the sudden decline that erases months of savings before there's time to react.

Direction is the wrong thing to model for that fear. Knowing a stock will probably close lower says nothing about whether it drops half a percent or twelve. What matters to someone holding the position is magnitude — how far down the floor is.

So the research question wasn't "will this go down." It was: are short-term drops genuinely unpredictable, or are there measurable signals that appear before downside risk rises? We defined the target as the maximum percentage a stock might fall over a forward five-day window, and modeled that magnitude directly for five large-cap technology names: Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Google, and Amazon.

That distribution is the whole problem in one chart. The bulk of the data is uninformative noise, and the events worth predicting sit in a thin tail. Any model that optimizes for the average day will fit the noise and ignore the tail.

Data and target construction

Price data came through the Alpha Vantage API for the five tickers, joined against quarterly earnings for the same companies. The choice of five liquid, heavily-traded large-caps was deliberate: it holds sector and liquidity roughly constant, so any signal the model finds is more likely to be about market conditions than about idiosyncratic differences between companies.

The target is forward-looking by construction. For each trading day, downside risk is the largest percentage decline realized over the following five sessions. That means every feature has to be computable from information available at that day — no lookahead, or the model learns to read the answer.

Building the warning system

Rather than watching price alone, we built a multivariate feature pipeline across three families of signal, each answering a different question about market state:

  • Momentum — how fast is price moving? RSI and MACD. The premise is that a name which has run up quickly has further to fall, and that momentum exhaustion precedes reversals.
  • Volatility — how unstable is the environment? Bollinger Band width and rolling standard deviation. Wider bands mean the market is already moving in larger increments, which mechanically raises the size of a plausible drop.
  • Fundamentals — does the price have support? Quarterly earnings, integrated to test whether company value backed the price level or whether the stock had detached from it.

The heatmap did double duty. It showed which features carried signal against the target, and it exposed the volatility measures as heavily correlated with one another — they describe the same underlying condition through different arithmetic. Knowing that mattered when interpreting coefficients later.

Simplicity wins

The expectation going in was that ensemble methods would win. Downside risk sounds like the kind of thing that should hide in interactions — a specific combination of overbought momentum and expanding volatility and weak earnings, rather than any one of them alone. Two models were tested against a linear baseline on that assumption:

  • Random forest — builds hundreds of decision trees on resampled data and averages them, which stabilizes predictions and captures non-linear splits a single tree would overfit.
  • Gradient boosting — builds models in sequence, each one trained specifically on the errors the previous one made, so the ensemble concentrates effort where it's currently wrong.

Both lost to linear regression.

The best model reached an R² of 0.526 — accounting for 52.6% of the variance in short-term downside risk — with predictions typically landing within 1.4 percentage points of the realized figure. For a five-day forward window on public technical indicators, that's a meaningful share of a quantity usually described as unforecastable.

Why the simple model won

The result is worth sitting with rather than filing as a curiosity. A plausible reading: the relationship between volatility state and forward downside magnitude is close to genuinely linear. Wider bands and higher realized volatility imply larger moves in both directions, and the size of the plausible drop scales with that more or less proportionally. There isn't an intricate interaction to find, so the models searching for one had nothing to gain and additional variance to lose.

The ensembles had more capacity to fit noise in a dataset where most observations are ordinary days. Extra flexibility is only an advantage when there's structure to flex toward.

The practical consequence is that the winning model is also the interpretable one. Each coefficient states how much forward downside risk moves per unit of its indicator. That is not a small thing when the output is meant to warn someone about their own money.

Why the result matters

Risk modeling of this kind usually sits inside a black box, where the reasoning behind a prediction is invisible to the person affected by it. A transparent linear model built on publicly available indicators — moving averages, RSI, volatility bands, volume behavior — is a plausible blueprint for a warning-light system that a retail investor could actually inspect and disagree with.

Where it breaks

Three limits worth stating plainly.

Feedback loops. If enough investors act on the same warning signal, the coordinated sell-off contributes to the decline the model was built to anticipate. A widely-adopted forecast changes the system it forecasts — the model's accuracy and its adoption are not independent.

Information inequality. The code is open; the high-frequency data feeds needed to run this live are not cheap. If only well-capitalized participants can afford the infrastructure, a tool framed as protective for retail investors ends up benefiting whoever already had the advantage.

Regime dependence. Five large-cap technology names over a finite window is a narrow slice. The linear relationship that held here was fit in a particular volatility regime, and the honest expectation is that it degrades in a crisis — precisely the condition where downside prediction would matter most. Testing across sectors and across a market dislocation is the obvious next step.

What I took from it

Two things. First, that model complexity is a hypothesis about the data, not a default setting — reaching for gradient boosting encodes a belief that the structure is non-linear, and that belief should be tested rather than assumed. Second, that in a financial context the interpretable model has value the accuracy metric doesn't capture. A number a person can trace back to a cause is worth more than a marginally better number they can't.

Data and tooling

Price and fundamentals data via the Alpha Vantage API. Python, pandas, and scikit-learn for feature engineering and modeling. AI tools were used to explore feature engineering approaches and to check the writing for clarity.
